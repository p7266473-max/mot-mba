--- Slide 3 ---
Introduction to Systems Thinking
The Systems Perspective
Organizations do not operate as isolated units. Every decision, process, technology platform, and stakeholder interaction forms part of a larger system.
Technology managers must understand how systems function and interact before selecting tools or developing solutions.
Discussion Question
If all modern infrastructure disappeared tomorrow, what would be the first system humanity would need to rebuild?
How do we coordinate resources without digital systems?

--- Slide 4 ---
Why Systems Thinking Matters
Key Concepts
Systems are deeply interconnected.
Local decisions affect global outcomes.
Actions create consequences; decisions generate feedback loops.
Small leverage-point changes can produce massive effects.
Management Insight
Managers frequently solve symptoms rather than underlying causes. Systems thinking helps decision-makers identify relationships, dependencies, and unintended consequences.
Case Scenario
A post-collapse society attempts to restart trade and coordination among survivors.
Question:
Which systems (communication, transport, ledger, resource tracking) must be rebuilt first to avoid chaos?

--- Slide 5 ---
What is a System?
Core Characteristics
Components: Interacting elements (people, process, tech).
Relationships: Connections and workflows between components.
Inputs & Outputs: Resources, data, or materials entering and leaving.
Boundaries: What is inside vs. outside the system's control.
Goals: The common objective the components work toward.
Real-World Examples
University, Hospital, Supply Chain, Banking and Transportation networks.
Explanation:
The overall performance of any system depends not only on its individual components but on how effectively they interact.

--- Slide 6 ---
Organizational Systems Thinking
Core System Components
People: Staff, stakeholders, and users.
Processes: Workflows and operational guidelines.
Technology: Hardware and software applications.
Information: Data, knowledge, and records.
Governance: Rules, policies, and decision authority.
Explanation
Organizations function as integrated systems where decisions in one department inevitably affect multiple departments.
Example: Introducing a New Payroll System
This single change affects multiple business units:
HR: Must update employee files and benefits structure.
Finance: Manages company cash flow and tax reporting.
Employees: Depend on accurate, timely payments (trust).
Management: Requires labor cost reporting for budgeting.
IT Operations: Must integrate, secure, and support the platform.

--- Slide 7 ---
Understanding Feedback Loops
Types of Feedback Loops
Positive (Reinforcing) Feedback
Drives growth reinforcement and expansion effects.
Can lead to exponential growth or runaway escalation.
Negative (Balancing) Feedback
Acts as a stability mechanism and error correction.
Maintains equilibrium (e.g., thermostat controlling temp).
Case Example: Panic Buying Loop
1. Food shortages create public panic.
2. Panic leads to aggressive hoarding (buying loop).
3. Hoarding creates even greater shortages.
This forms an escalating reinforcing loop that collapses the system.

--- Slide 13 ---
Technology Management Planning
Definition
Technology planning ensures that technology investments directly align with organizational goals.
Core Objectives
1. Solve real business problems.
2. Improve operational efficiency.
3. Support overall strategy.
4. Reduce implementation risks.
Common Planning Mistakes
Technology-First Thinking: Buying tools before identifying the problem.
Solution Before Problem: Forcing a specific software platform because it's popular.
Vendor-Driven Decisions: Allowing software providers to define the business needs.
The Correct Planning Flow
1. Understand the problem.
2. Gather requirements.
3. Evaluate alternatives.
4. Select the aligned solution.

Non-functional requirements define HOW a system should operate (quality constraints).

Scalability: The ledger can grow from 100 to 10,000 users without crashing.

System design transforms gathered requirements into a structured, implementable blueprint.
Key Activities

Process Design: Mapping workflows and data paths.

User Interface: Designing user interaction screens.

Information: Data, transactions, and logs.
Processes: Standard operating procedures.
Technology: Hardware, networks, and databases.
Rules: Business policies and compliance.

Key Characteristics
Sequential: One phase ends before the next begins.
Structured: Highly predictable with clear milestones.

Week 5 – SDLC
What is SDLC? — System Development Life Cycle
Definition
SDLC is a structured management approach used to plan, design, build, test, and maintain large-scale information systems.
Why 'Life Cycle'?
Because systems are not built once and forgotten. Like living organisms, they are designed, launched, updated, and eventually retired — in a controlled cycle.
What SDLC Defines
▸  HOW systems are planned before any development begins
▸  HOW requirements are gathered from stakeholders
▸  HOW systems are designed, coded, and built
▸  HOW they are tested for correctness and quality
▸  HOW they are deployed to real users
▸  HOW they are maintained and improved over time
In short: SDLC prevents chaos in large-scale system development.

--- Slide 5 ---
Slide 5
Week 5 – SDLC
Why SDLC Matters in an Enterprise Context
The Scale Problem
In a small office system, a mistake costs one team a few hours. In a national ERP serving 5 million people, a mistake can disrupt food supply, salary payments, or medical records.
SDLC Is the Answer
▸  Provides a repeatable, disciplined process for building systems
▸  Reduces risk of project failure (which costs billions globally)
▸  Ensures stakeholder alignment at every phase
▸  Creates documentation that supports future changes and audits
Real-World Relevance
According to the Standish Group CHAOS Report, over 66% of large IT projects fail or face significant issues — most due to poor SDLC discipline.
▸  ERP implementations at SAP, Oracle, or Microsoft all follow SDLC
▸  Government systems (tax, health, identity) are built using SDLC
▸  Banking core systems undergo rigorous SDLC phases

--- Slide 6 ---
Slide 6
Week 5 – SDLC
SDLC Phases — The Building Blocks of Every System
Phase 1 — Planning
Define scope, budget, timeline, and feasibility. What are we building and can we afford it?
Phase 2 — Requirements Analysis
Gather what the system must do. Interview stakeholders. Define functional and non-functional requirements.
Phase 3 — System Design
Create architecture blueprints. Choose technology. Define database structure, interfaces, and modules.
Phase 4 — Development
Programmers build the system according to the design. This is the coding phase.
Phase 5 — Testing
Verify the system works correctly. Find and fix bugs before deployment.
Phase 6 — Deployment
Release the system to real users in a controlled rollout.
Phase 7 — Maintenance
Monitor, update, and improve the system continuously over its lifetime.

--- Slide 7 ---
Slide 8
Week 5 – SDLC Models
SDLC Models — Not One Size Fits All
Why Multiple Models?
SDLC defines WHAT phases to follow. Models define HOW those phases are executed in sequence.
Different systems have different characteristics — stable or dynamic, low-risk or high-risk, simple or complex. Each needs a different execution style.
The Four Major SDLC Models
▸  Waterfall Model — for stable, well-defined systems
▸  Spiral Model — for high-risk, complex systems
▸  Prototyping Model — for experimental, unclear systems
▸  Agile Model — for fast-changing, iterative systems
How We Choose a Model
The choice depends on: clarity of requirements, risk tolerance, time pressure, and stakeholder involvement needed.
In our kingdom, ALL FOUR models are in use simultaneously for different subsystems.

Slide 9
Week 5 – Waterfall
Waterfall Model — Building Stable, Fixed Systems
What Is the Waterfall Model?
A sequential, phase-by-phase approach where each step must be fully completed before the next begins. Like water flowing downhill — it only goes one direction.
When to Use Waterfall
▸  Requirements are completely clear and unlikely to change
▸  The system is stable, well-understood, and policy-governed
▸  Regulatory approval is needed at each phase
Kingdom Application
▸  Fixed currency exchange rate system (set at 2001 rates)
▸  Gold valuation and national reserve management rules
▸  Tax policy rules and national budget allocation structure
Advantages
▸  Simple, predictable, well-documented
▸  Easy to manage and audit
Limitations
▸  Very inflexible — changes are costly and disruptive
▸  Poor choice for dynamic or uncertain environments

--- Slide 9 ---
Slide 10
Week 5 – Spiral
Spiral Model — Managing Risk in Complex Systems
What Is the Spiral Model?
An iterative development approach where each cycle (spiral) includes: planning, risk analysis, building a component, and evaluating results. The process repeats until the system is complete.
The Four Quadrants of Each Spiral
▸  Planning: Define objectives for this iteration
▸  Risk Analysis: Identify and mitigate risks before building
▸  Development: Build and test the component for this cycle
▸  Evaluation: Review with stakeholders before the next spiral
Kingdom Application
▸  Commodity pricing engine (subject to seasonal changes)
▸  Food supply chain management system
▸  Agricultural production and harvest forecasting
▸  Emergency resource allocation during crises
Key Advantage
Risk is addressed early and continuously, making it ideal for large, uncertain projects.

Week 5 – Prototyping
Prototyping Model — Experimenting Before Committing
What Is the Prototyping Model?
Rather than building a full system, developers first build a simplified working model (prototype) that users can test and provide feedback on. The real system is then built based on what was learned.
Why Prototyping Is Powerful
Many organizations don't fully know what they want until they see something working. Prototyping surfaces hidden requirements and reduces the risk of building the wrong system.
The Prototyping Cycle
▸  Gather initial requirements (broad, not detailed)
▸  Build a quick, working prototype
▸  Let users test and provide feedback
▸  Refine the prototype based on feedback
▸  Repeat until requirements are clear
▸  Build the final production system
Kingdom Application
▸  GPT-based AI citizen query interface (experimental NLP system)
▸  Citizen digital ID portal (new concept requiring user validation)

--- Slide 11 ---

Week 5 – Agile
Agile Model — Speed, Iteration, and Collaboration
What Is Agile?
Agile is an iterative development methodology where systems are built in short cycles called 'sprints' (typically 2–4 weeks each). Each sprint produces a working, deployable piece of the system.
Agile Core Principles
▸  Working software over heavy documentation
▸  Customer collaboration over contract negotiation
▸  Responding to change over following a fixed plan
▸  Individuals and interactions over processes and tools
The Sprint Cycle
▸  Sprint Planning → Sprint Execution → Review → Retrospective → Next Sprint
Kingdom Application
▸  Main ERP system development (finance, HR, housing modules)
▸  Citizen-facing government service portals
▸  AI feature enhancements for the GPT interface
Agile is the most widely used model in modern enterprise software development.

Week 5 – Summary
Comparing the Four SDLC Models — Decision Guide
When to Use Each Model
▸  WATERFALL → Use when: Requirements are fixed, system is stable, regulations require sequential sign-off
▸  SPIRAL → Use when: High risk, complex system, iterative risk management needed
▸  PROTOTYPING → Use when: Requirements are unclear, user feedback is critical, new/experimental systems
▸  AGILE → Use when: Requirements change frequently, speed of delivery is key, strong stakeholder involvement
Key Differentiators
Waterfall is predictable but rigid. Spiral is thorough but slow. Prototyping is exploratory but can drift. Agile is fast but requires discipline.
Kingdom Model Mapping
▸  Currency System → Waterfall (fixed rules)
▸  Commodity Pricing → Spiral (dynamic risk)
▸  AI Interface → Prototyping (experimental)
▸  ERP Core Modules → Agile (fast iteration)

Week 5 – Requirements
Requirements Engineering — Getting It Right Before Building
What Are Requirements?
Requirements are detailed descriptions of what a system must do and how it must perform. They form the contract between the business and the technology team.
Two Types of Requirements
▸  Functional Requirements: What the system does (features, functions, rules)
▸  Non-Functional Requirements: How well it does it (speed, security, reliability)
Why Requirements Matter
Research shows that 60-70% of system failures are caused by poor or missing requirements — NOT by poor coding. Getting requirements right is a management responsibility, not just a technical one.
Kingdom Example
▸  Functional: System must process citizen salary payments within 24 hours
▸  Functional: Health module must store medical records for all 5M citizens
▸  Non-Functional: System must maintain 99.9% uptime for critical services
▸  Non-Functional: All citizen data must be encrypted at rest and in transit

Week 5 – Design
System Design — Blueprint Before Construction
What is System Design?
System design is the process of converting requirements into a detailed technical blueprint. It defines the architecture, modules, databases, interfaces, and data flows BEFORE any building begins.
Think of it Like Building Architecture
A civil architect creates detailed blueprints before any construction begins. A system designer creates architecture diagrams and technical specifications before any coding begins.
What System Design Includes
▸  Architecture design: How the system is structured at a high level
▸  Database design: What data is stored and how it is organized
▸  Interface design: How users interact with the system
▸  Integration design: How different modules talk to each other
▸  Security design: How the system is protected
Kingdom Design Example
▸  ERP hub-and-spoke architecture connecting all 10 domain modules
▸  Central database with citizen ID as the master reference key

Week 5 – ERP
What is ERP? — Enterprise Resource Planning Explained
Definition
ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) is a unified software system that integrates all core business functions into a single platform — finance, human resources, supply chain, operations, and more.
Before ERP: The Siloed World
Before ERP, departments used separate, disconnected systems. Finance didn't see what Procurement was doing. HR didn't connect to Payroll. This caused data duplication, errors, and poor decision-making.
After ERP: One System of Truth
ERP creates a single source of truth. All departments share the same data, in real time, through one integrated platform.
Real-World Examples
▸  SAP — used by most Fortune 500 companies globally
▸  Oracle ERP Cloud — used by governments and multinational firms
▸  Microsoft Dynamics — used by mid-size enterprises
In the Kingdom, a custom ERP manages all national operations — replacing pre-war fragmented paper systems.

Week 5 – JSON Schema
JSON Schema — The Data Quality Rule Engine
What is JSON Schema? (In Plain Language)
JSON Schema is a rule system that ensures data is correctly structured, complete, and consistent before it enters or leaves a system. Think of it as a customs checkpoint — only properly formatted data is allowed through.
Why ERP Systems Need It
▸  Without rules: A salary field might be left blank, or a date entered incorrectly
▸  Without rules: An AI might generate a fictional citizen ID that doesn't exist
▸  Without rules: Financial records become unreliable and unauditable
What JSON Schema Does
▸  Ensures all required fields are present (no missing data)
▸  Validates that values match expected formats (numbers, dates, IDs)
▸  Prevents out-of-range values (e.g., negative population count)
▸  Validates AI-generated outputs before they reach the ERP
Think of It Like a Government Form
A tax form requires specific fields in specific formats. JSON Schema is the digital equivalent — a template that all data must match before being accepted.

Week 6 – ITIL
ITIL — IT Infrastructure Library (Service Management)
What Is ITIL?
ITIL is the world's most widely adopted IT service management framework. It provides a set of detailed practices for planning, delivering, and continuously improving IT services.
The Core ITIL Concept
IT should be treated as a SERVICE to the organization — like electricity or water. It must be reliable, consistent, and continuously improved. When it fails, there must be defined processes to fix it.
ITIL's Five Service Stages
▸  Service Strategy: What services do we need? What value do they deliver?
▸  Service Design: How do we design services to meet requirements?
▸  Service Transition: How do we safely move services into production?
▸  Service Operation: How do we run services reliably day-to-day?
▸  Continual Service Improvement (CSI): How do we measure and improve continuously?
Kingdom Application

Week 6 – ITIL
ITIL Key Processes — How It Works in Practice
Incident Management
When a system goes down or malfunctions, the Incident Management process ensures it is quickly identified, escalated, and resolved. In the kingdom: if the salary payment system fails, ITIL Incident Management triggers an immediate response.
Change Management
No change is made to a production system without approval. Every software update, database change, or configuration modification goes through a formal Change Advisory Board (CAB) review.
Problem Management
When incidents recur frequently, Problem Management investigates the ROOT CAUSE, not just the symptom. This prevents the same failure from happening again.
Service Level Management
Formal agreements (SLAs) define the performance standards the IT team must meet. For example: 'The citizen portal must be available 99.9% of the time' or 'Incidents must be resolved within 4 hours.'
Configuration Management
A complete inventory (CMDB) of all IT assets, their relationships, and their current state is maintained. This is critical for rapid troubleshooting.

COBIT — Governance and Control of IT
What Is COBIT?
COBIT (Control Objectives for Information and Related Technologies) is an IT governance framework developed by ISACA. It bridges the gap between technical IT operations and business strategy, ensuring IT investments deliver measurable value.
The Central COBIT Question

▸  MEA — Monitor, Evaluate & Assess: Performance measurement and audit

TOGAF — Enterprise Architecture Framework
What Is TOGAF?
TOGAF (The Open Group Architecture Framework) is the most widely used enterprise architecture methodology globally. It provides a structured approach to designing, planning, and governing the overall IT landscape of an organization.

Week 6 – Change Mgmt
IT Change Management — Controlling System Evolution
Why Is Change Management Critical?
The most dangerous moment for any IT system is when it is being changed. Studies show that over 80% of major IT outages are caused by poorly managed changes — not by external attacks.
What Is IT Change Management?
A formal process that controls how changes are proposed, approved, tested, implemented, and reviewed. No change happens without following this process.
The Change Management Process
▸  Change Request (RFC): Formally document the proposed change
▸  Impact Assessment: What systems and users will be affected?
▸  CAB Review: Change Advisory Board evaluates risk and approves/rejects
▸  Test Deployment: Change is tested in a non-production environment first
▸  Production Deployment: Approved change is implemented in controlled window
▸  Post-Implementation Review: Was the change successful? Any issues?
Kingdom Example
▸  Updating the currency exchange module requires CAB approval, security testing, stakeholder notification, a rollback plan, and post-deployment validation

Week 6 – Risk Mgmt
IT Risk Management — Identifying and Mitigating Threats
What Is IT Risk?
IT risk is the potential for technology failures, security breaches, data loss, or system outages to cause harm to the organization or its stakeholders.
The Risk Management Process
▸  Identify: What could go wrong? (List all potential risks)
▸  Assess: How likely is it? How severe would the impact be?
▸  Prioritize: Focus resources on highest-impact, highest-probability risks
▸  Mitigate: What actions reduce the risk to acceptable levels?
▸  Monitor: Continuously watch for new risks and track mitigation effectiveness
Risk Categories for the Kingdom ERP
▸  Operational Risk: System downtime disrupting citizen services
▸  Security Risk: Unauthorized access to citizen financial data
▸  Data Risk: Corruption or loss of critical national records
▸  Compliance Risk: Failure to meet privacy and governance regulations
▸  Technology Risk: Vendor failure or obsolescence of key systems
Risk Appetite
Organizations define their 'risk appetite' — how much risk they are willing to accept. In the Kingdom, zero tolerance for health and financial data risks.

Week 6 – Security
IT Security Architecture — Protecting the Digital Kingdom
Why Security Architecture?
Security is not a single tool or product — it is a layered system of controls that work together to protect data, systems, and services from threats.
The Defense-in-Depth Model
Like a medieval castle with multiple defensive walls, IT security uses multiple layers. If one layer is breached, the next layer still provides protection.
Security Layers
▸  Perimeter Security: Firewalls, DMZ (demilitarized zone), Intrusion Detection Systems
▸  Network Security: Encryption of data in transit, VPN for secure access
▸  Application Security: Authentication, authorization, input validation
▸  Data Security: Encryption at rest, data masking, access control
Key Security Principles
▸  Confidentiality: Only authorized parties can access data
▸  Integrity: Data cannot be modified without authorization
▸  Availability: Systems remain accessible when needed (CIA Triad)

▸  Citizen medical and financial records require highest security classification

Week 6 – BCP
Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery
What Is Business Continuity Planning (BCP)?
BCP is the process of ensuring that critical organizational functions can continue operating during and after a major disruption — whether caused by a technical failure, cyberattack, natural disaster, or human error.
What Is Disaster Recovery (DR)?
DR is the specific technical plan for how IT systems are restored after a major failure. It is a subset of BCP focused on technology recovery.
Key Metrics
▸  RTO — Recovery Time Objective: How quickly must the system be restored?
▸  RPO — Recovery Point Objective: How much data loss is acceptable?
Kingdom BCP Priorities
▸  Priority 1 (RTO < 1 hour): Citizen health records, emergency services
▸  Priority 2 (RTO < 4 hours): Payroll, food distribution, energy management
▸  Priority 3 (RTO < 24 hours): Administrative systems, reporting
BCP Infrastructure Requirements
▸  Hot standby data centre: Fully operational backup system at all times
▸  Geographic distribution: Backup centre in different physical location
▸  Regular backup and restore drills to verify recovery capability

Week 6 – SLA
Service Level Management — Measuring IT Performance
What Is a Service Level Agreement (SLA)?
An SLA is a formal agreement between the IT team and the business that defines the minimum acceptable performance standards for each IT service.
Why SLAs Matter for Managers
Without SLAs, IT performance is subjective. With SLAs, performance is measurable, accountable, and enforceable. Managers use SLAs to hold IT teams accountable and to justify IT investment.
Typical SLA Metrics
▸  Availability: 99.9% uptime = no more than 8.76 hours downtime per year
▸  Response Time: Critical incidents responded to within 15 minutes
▸  Resolution Time: High-priority incidents resolved within 4 hours
▸  Data Backup: Daily backups completed with 99.99% success rate
Kingdom SLA Example
▸  Citizen salary payment system: 99.95% monthly availability
▸  Health records access: 24/7 availability with maximum 5-minute degraded service
▸  Currency exchange rate engine: Real-time updates with < 2 second latency
Consequences of SLA Breach
Financial penalties, leadership accountability, mandatory root cause analysis, and remediation plans.

Intranet vs. Extranet: Fundamentals
Distinguishing security zones in e-business networking
E-businesses use segmented networks to balance collaborative efficiency with cybersecurity compliance:
•  Intranet: A private network accessible only to internal employees. Used to share HR forms, corporate policies, and collaboration tools.
•  Extranet: An extension of the intranet that allows authorized external partners, clients, or suppliers to access limited network zones.
•  Firewall Control: Delineates borders between the internal LAN, the DMZ (extranet portals), and the public internet.
•  Identity Management: Leverages Single Sign-On (SSO) and VPN paths to manage access permissions.

--- Slide 12 ---
Intranet vs. Extranet Network Setup
Visualizing firewall barriers and DMZ segments
Secure network design uses firewall-isolated zones to keep internal databases separated from public web applications:
•  Intranet (LAN): Protected from the public internet. Houses sensitive databases.
•  Extranet (DMZ): Sits between public web and internal core systems, handling incoming B2B partner links.
•  VPN/HTTPS: Encrypts connections for external contractors accessing extranet nodes.

Change Management in E-Business
Navigating technological and organizational transitions
Successful application deployment requires active change management to align users with new tools:
•  Concept Definition: A structured approach to transition individuals, teams, and organizations to a desired future state.
•  The Human Factor: Recognizes that system failure is often caused by staff resistance, not technical bugs.
•  Lewin's Stage Model: Unfreeze (preparing for change) -> Change (migrating processes) -> Refreeze (locking in new habits).
•  ADKAR Framework: Builds Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, and Reinforcement to guide individuals through changes.

A linear model for guiding individual system adoption

•  Awareness: Clearly communicate the reasons behind the old system's retirement.
•  Desire: Address team concerns to build support for the incoming application.
•  Reinforcement: Use rewards and feedback loops to prevent staff from reverting to old spreadsheets.

--- Slide 31 ---
Business Continuity & Disaster Recovery
Ensuring application resilience against critical disruptions
System managers design redundancy and failover paths to protect operations from critical data loss:
•  Business Continuity (BC): Processes that keep key operations running during an outage (e.g. power loss, ransomware).
•  Disaster Recovery (DR): Technical recovery plans focused on restoring database systems and IT servers.
•  Recovery Time Objective (RTO): The maximum acceptable duration a system can remain offline before causing severe damage.
•  Recovery Point Objective (RPO): The maximum age of data that must be recovered from backup storage for operations to resume.

--- Slide 32 ---
Business Continuity Lifecycle
A continuous model for maintaining system resilience
Building organizational resilience requires a continuous lifecycle of risk assessments and recovery tests:
•  Impact Analysis (BIA): Identify which application outages will cause immediate financial damage.
•  Redundancy Strategy: Set up database replication and backup hosting locations.
•  Testing & Simulations: Regularly run dry-runs of data recovery scripts to identify flaws.
